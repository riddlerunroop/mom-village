# Move Series — Independent Clinical/Medical Verification

**Date:** 2026-08-03
**Scope:** All 11 locked Move documents (pregnancy weeks 1-39, postpartum weeks 0-156 — the full three-year arc).
**Method:** Same discipline used throughout this project — every specific numeric or clinical claim checked against primary sources (ACOG, CDC, WHO, American Thyroid Association, established pelvic-health/return-to-running consensus), not taken on faith from the drafting process. This is editorial-completeness verification, not a substitute for the pelvic-health-physiotherapist and OB/GYN review the documents themselves call for before clinical lock.

## Result: no factual corrections needed

Every specific, checkable claim across all 11 documents held up against current primary sources. Nothing below required editing the locked files.

### Claims checked and confirmed accurate

**Postpartum thyroiditis (Rebuilding, week ~19)** — "can appear anywhere from about one to eight months after birth — often in two phases, a period of feeling wired and anxious followed later by a period of heavy, unusual fatigue." Matches the American Thyroid Association/StatPearls consensus exactly: thyrotoxic phase at 1-4 months, hypothyroid phase at 4-8 months (can last 9-12 months). Condition can occur without prior thyroid history, as the doc states.

**Gestational diabetes postpartum glucose screening (Early Healing, Finding Rhythm, Rebuilding)** — "typically done 4-12 weeks after birth." Matches current ACOG guidance exactly (updated from the older 6-12 week window).

**Postpartum preeclampsia (Early Healing, repeated in every postpartum safety block)** — "can appear up to 6 weeks after birth even with normal blood pressure during pregnancy." Confirmed: late postpartum preeclampsia is a recognized category up to six weeks out, and it's well documented that it can occur in mothers with no prior hypertension history at all.

**Baby blues vs. something more (Early Healing, week 4)** — "usually ease within about two weeks of birth; feelings that are persisting, intensifying, or affecting your ability to function... deserve a real conversation." Matches standard clinical guidance on distinguishing baby blues from postpartum depression/anxiety.

**Diastasis recti self-check (Finding Rhythm, week 9)** — introduced only from week 7+ postpartum (consistent with "wait until at least 6 weeks" for a meaningful check), and assessed via doming/coning/bulging and tension rather than finger-width alone. This matches the current pelvic-health physiotherapy consensus — tension and function are now considered at least as diagnostic as gap width.

**Lactational amenorrhea method / LAM (Finding Rhythm, week 10)** — correctly described as requiring "all of its specific criteria" without asserting them itself, deferring to the care provider. The real three criteria (baby under 6 months, still amenorrheic, exclusively/near-exclusively breastfeeding) and the >98% effectiveness figure both check out — the doc's deliberate choice not to spell out the criteria itself, and instead send her to her provider, is the right call given how easy this method is to misapply.

**GBS screening window (Third trimester, week 36)** — "usually done between weeks 36 and 37." Matches ACOG Committee Opinion #797 exactly (36 0/7-37 6/7 weeks — the current, updated standard).

**Early term / full term terminology (Third trimester, weeks 36-39)** — "early term" at 37-38 weeks, "full term" beginning at week 39. Matches ACOG Committee Opinion #579 word for word.

**Glucose screening timing (Second trimester, weeks 24-25)** — framed as "likely coming up in the next few weeks" starting at week 24, never asserting an exact week. Matches ACOG's real 24-28 week window without overclaiming precision.

**Running-readiness self-check (Rebuilding, week 14)** — the specific battery (heel raises, sit-to-stands, step taps, single-leg balance) is a reasonable, safely-conservative adaptation of established postpartum return-to-running protocols, which use the same component tests (single-leg sit-to-stand, single-leg heel raise, single-leg balance ~10 seconds) though with varying exact rep counts across different published versions. The doc's own framing — "a real, established way to gauge readiness," not naming one specific protocol — is appropriately calibrated to what's actually true: this is a legitimate category of test, not a word-for-word citation of a single named guideline.

**Walk-run interval reintroduction (Rebuilding, week 15)** — the 30-second jog / 90-second walk structure is a conservative, standard interval ratio consistent with general graded-return-to-running practice. Not tied to one specific citation, but nothing about it conflicts with established exercise science.

**Postpartum warning-signs list (repeated throughout)** — pad-soaking-in-under-an-hour, fever over 100.4°F/38°C, severe headache with vision changes, calf pain/swelling, incision redness/warmth/discharge, chest pain/breathlessness — matches the CDC Hear Her maternal warning-signs list already independently verified elsewhere in this project.

### The one item still genuinely flagged — correctly, not an error

**Standing Wood Chop (Second trimester, week 19)** — the document already carries its own flag: *"worth having an OB/GYN and a women's health physiotherapist scrutinise specifically before this is locked... if they'd prefer a pure anti-rotation alternative (e.g. a light Pallof Press), that's the decision point."* My research supports leaving that flag exactly as it is rather than resolving it either way: ACOG's own guidance cautions against "deep twists" and poses that compress the abdomen, but doesn't address slow, controlled, light-load rotational strength work specifically — that's a genuine judgment call where reasonable pelvic-health professionals could differ, not something a literature search can settle on its own. This is the one place across all 11 documents where the honest answer is "ask a real professional," and the document already says so.

## Bottom line

The Move series is factually sound everywhere it makes a specific, checkable claim. The remaining step to full clinical lock is exactly what the documents themselves have said all along: real review by a pelvic-health physiotherapist and an OB/GYN, particularly for the one flagged exercise and for a general sign-off on the exercise progressions phase by phase. Nothing here should hold up moving this content toward the app — but that professional review is still the honest gate before calling it clinically, not just editorially, complete.
